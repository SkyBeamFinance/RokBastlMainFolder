Advanced Report: Language Impairment in Native Tongue and Preservation in Second Language in an Individual with Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (C-PTSD) Introduction This report examines a complex clinical presentation in a 33-year-old male with a de facto diagnosis of Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (C-PTSD) who exhibits significant functional impairment and subjective distress when using his native language (Slovene), including experiencing "abhorrent" cognitive function when thinking in it, while maintaining relative fluency and clarity of thought in his second language (English). Furthermore, this individual displays a paradoxical reaction, finding declarations of love from a potentially abusive maternal figure humorous. This phenomenon, while not universally documented, can be understood through the lens of current research on C-PTSD, trauma's impact on the brain and cognition, bilingual language processing, dissociation, and distorted attachment dynamics.
1. Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (C-PTSD): Cognitive and Emotional
Impact C-PTSD is a severe form of trauma response resulting from prolonged, repeated, and often interpersonal trauma, typically occurring in contexts where escape is difficult or impossible, such as in childhood abuse or neglect [1, 3]. Unlike single-incident PTSD, C-PTSD involves profound disturbances in affect regulation, self-concept, and interpersonal relationships, in addition to core PTSD symptoms like re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal [1, 3.2]. These disturbances are associated with significant neurobiological and cognitive changes.
Research indicates that C-PTSD is linked to impairments in various cognitive domains, including attention, memory, executive functions (such as planning, judgment, and cognitive flexibility), and processing speed [3.2, 3.3]. The chronic stress and dysregulation associated with C-PTSD can impact brain structures involved in these functions, particularly the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala [3.4]. The amygdala, the brain's fear center, may become hyperactive, leading to a state of heightened alert and reactivity, while the prefrontal cortex, responsible for executive control and regulation, may show reduced activity [3.4]. This neurobiological profile contributes to difficulties in organizing thoughts, regulating emotions, and maintaining cognitive control, all of which are essential for effective language processing.
Emotional dysregulation is a hallmark of C-PTSD [1, 3.1]. Individuals may experience intense, labile emotions, difficulty identifying and expressing feelings, and resort to maladaptive coping strategies. This constant state of emotional turmoil places a

2. Language Processing in Bilinguals and the Impact of Trauma
Bilingualism involves complex cognitive processes related to managing and switching between two language systems [4.2, 4.4]. While the exact neural representation of two languages is still an area of active research, it is understood that both languages are active to some degree, and the brain employs control mechanisms to select the target language and inhibit the non-target language [4.2, 4.4].
The impact of trauma on language processing in bilingual individuals is a growing area of study. Emerging evidence suggests that processing traumatic memories in a second language can lead to reduced emotional reactivity compared to processing them in the native language [2.2, 2.5, 3.4]. This "foreign language effect" on emotion is thought to occur because the second language may be less deeply connected to the visceral and emotional experiences of early life and trauma [2.2, 3.4]. The native language, being the language of early attachment and potentially the language in which traumatic events occurred, is often more intimately linked to emotional and bodily states [2.3].
For an individual with C-PTSD, the native language (Slovene) may be deeply intertwined with the traumatic experiences, serving as a powerful trigger for re-experiencing symptoms, intense negative emotions, and a sense of being overwhelmed. The sounds, structures, or even specific words in the native language could be unconsciously associated with traumatic memories and the accompanying physiological and emotional distress. This strong affective link can disrupt the smooth and efficient processing of the native language for cognitive tasks.
Conversely, the second language (English), acquired later and potentially in a less threatening environment, may lack these deep, trauma-related emotional associations.
Using English could provide a degree of emotional distance or buffering, allowing the individual to engage in cognitive processes with less interference from trauma-related triggers and dysregulation [2.2, 3.4]. This emotional detachment in the second language may facilitate clearer thinking and more organized expression, making it a more functional linguistic tool in the aftermath of trauma.
Furthermore, some theories suggest that first language acquisition relies more on implicit, procedural memory, while second language acquisition, particularly in adulthood, involves more explicit, declarative processes [1.4]. If C-PTSD has differentially impacted these memory systems or the brain regions that support them, it could potentially contribute to the observed discrepancy in native versus second

3. Dissociation and Language Fragmentation
Dissociation is a key feature of C-PTSD and a survival mechanism in response to overwhelming trauma [2.1, 2.2]. It involves a disruption in the integrated processing of information, leading to a disconnection from aspects of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, and sensation [2.2, 2.4]. In the context of chronic trauma, dissociation can lead to a fragmentation of the self and a compartmentalization of traumatic memories and associated emotions.
Dissociation can profoundly impact language processing. Traumatic memories, especially when dissociated, are often stored in a fragmented, sensory-based format rather than a coherent narrative [2.2]. Accessing or attempting to articulate these memories, particularly in the language in which the trauma occurred, can trigger dissociative states or an influx of fragmented, overwhelming material that disrupts coherent thought and speech.
For the individual in question, using his native Slovene might activate these dissociated trauma states. The language itself could be a trigger, leading to: ●​ Cognitive Disintegration: The "abhorrent" function when thinking in Slovene could be a subjective experience of the mind struggling to integrate fragmented thoughts, emotions, and memories that are being triggered by the language. Dissociation can interfere with the normal flow of thought and association. ●​ Emotional Overload: The native language may be so deeply saturated with the emotional intensity of the trauma that attempting to use it for neutral or complex thought becomes overwhelming, leading to a breakdown in cognitive function. ●​ Disconnection from Embodied Experience: Trauma and dissociation can lead to a disconnection from the body and its sensations [3.5]. If the native language is strongly linked to the embodied experience of trauma, using it might trigger this disconnection, further disrupting the integrated cognitive-linguistic process.
The second language, English, may serve as a linguistic space less burdened by these dissociative triggers and fragmented trauma states. It might allow the individual to access a more integrated sense of self or process information with less interference from the dissociative barriers erected around the traumatic core, thus facilitating clearer thinking.
4. The Paradoxical Reaction to "Love" and Hypothetical Harm

a powerful indicator of severely distorted attachment and the psychological impact of betrayal trauma within C-PTSD. This seemingly paradoxical response is not indicative of finding harm or lack of love genuinely amusing, but rather serves as a complex defense mechanism against unbearable psychological pain and cognitive dissonance.
Several factors contribute to this reaction: ●​ Profound Incongruity and Absurdity: The core of the humor lies in the extreme and grotesque contradiction between the words "I love you deeply" and the reality of the mother's capacity for harm and betrayal, especially in the chilling hypothetical scenario. For someone who has experienced profound trauma and betrayal from a primary caregiver, the concept of "love" from that source is likely warped and associated with pain, fear, or manipulation. The declaration of deep love, when contrasted with the potential for ultimate harm, is so fundamentally absurd and horrifyingly inconsistent that the mind may react with a form of dark, cynical humor as a defense against the overwhelming cognitive dissonance and emotional pain. ●​ Betrayal of Trust and Meaning: Trauma inflicted by an attachment figure fundamentally violates the expectation of safety and care associated with love. The words "I love you" become associated with this betrayal. Finding them funny is a bitter, jaded reaction to the perceived hollowness or manipulative nature of these declarations in the context of the actual relationship history. It reflects a profound loss of faith in the meaning of love from that source. ●​ Emotional Numbing and Detachment (Dissociation): Laughter in this context can be a manifestation of emotional numbing or detachment, a form of dissociation.
The individual intellectually registers the words "I love you" but is disconnected from the positive emotional response they would typically evoke. The words trigger a sense of unreality or absurdity because they do not align with the deeply ingrained experience of the relationship as unsafe and unloving. The humor is a response to this emotional void and the perceived disconnect between the spoken words and the reality of the relationship. ●​ Dark Humor as a Coping Mechanism: As mentioned earlier, dark humor is a common coping mechanism for trauma survivors. It allows individuals to express the horror, absurdity, and pain of their experiences in a way that can feel less overwhelming than direct emotional expression. In this case, the humor is a way of processing the unbearable reality of a mother figure who is both the source of trauma and yet makes declarations of love. ●​ Reaction to Perceived Manipulation or Theatricality: If the mother exhibits traits of HPD, her declarations of love might be perceived as overly dramatic, performative, or manipulative, designed to elicit a reaction or maintain control rather than express genuine affection. The humor could be a cynical reaction to this perceived theatricality and lack of authenticity, especially when juxtaposed with the

Conclusion The described linguistic and emotional phenomena in this individual with C-PTSD are likely the result of a complex interplay between the neurobiological and cognitive impacts of chronic trauma, the specific dynamics of a severely dysfunctional attachment relationship, the nature of bilingual language processing, and the use of dissociation as a survival mechanism. The native language, deeply intertwined with the traumatic past and fragmented internal states, becomes a trigger for distress and cognitive impairment.
The second language, less burdened by these associations, offers a linguistic and cognitive space for greater clarity and emotional distance. The paradoxical reaction of finding declarations of love from the abusive figure humorous is a powerful, albeit painful, defense mechanism reflecting the profound betrayal of trust and the distortion of the meaning of love within the context of complex trauma. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for providing trauma-informed care and therapeutic interventions that address the multifaceted impact of C-PTSD on an individual's internal world and their relationship with language.
